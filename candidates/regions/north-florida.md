# North Florida / Florida Panhandle

**Priority: 5 (stretch)**

## Why this region

Florida has the largest and most reliable springs in the country — first-magnitude springs (>65 million gallons per day) that flow steadily year-round from the Floridan Aquifer. If springs are the top priority and budget is the only constraint, this is the geological gold standard.

The reason it's a stretch goal, not a primary target: Florida land prices for 1,000+ acre tracts typically exceed $4,000/acre, and parcels with springs often carry conservation easements, water-management-district restrictions, or zoning related to spring protection — any of which fail G4 or G6.

## Geology

Flat-lying Eocene-Oligocene limestone (the Floridan Aquifer system) under thin sandy soils. The aquifer is enormous, recharged by 50–60 inches of annual rainfall, and discharges through springs along the rivers (Suwannee, Santa Fe, Ichetucknee, Wakulla, Wacissa). First-magnitude springs include Wakulla, Silver, Ichetucknee, Manatee, Rainbow, Alapaha Rise.

Budget of $4M generally buys ~600–1,200 acres here. The 1,000-acre + spring + budget combination is genuinely difficult.

## Zoning posture

**This is the second binding constraint.** Florida counties are almost universally zoned, and several have specific overlay zoning for springs protection (Suwannee County's Springs Protection Zone, Marion County's Farmland Preservation Area, etc.). Many otherwise-attractive parcels fail G6 just from county zoning.

Counties to look at first (less restrictive, still spring-bearing): Lafayette, Madison, Hamilton, Liberty, Calhoun. All are zoned, so verify the specific parcel zoning permits the intended use.

## Mineral rights climate

Generally favorable for surface use — Florida is not a major hydrocarbon producer. Phosphate mining is a concern in central FL (Polk, Hardee cos.) but largely south of the spring-rich north Florida region. Mineral title is usually clean.

The bigger water-rights question in Florida is **the consumptive-use permit**. Even if you own the spring, withdrawing more than a small amount of water requires a permit from the relevant Water Management District (Suwannee River WMD, Northwest Florida WMD). This is not a G-gate question but it affects feasibility for any irrigation-intensive use.

## Spring inventory

- Florida DEP maintains a comprehensive springs database with first- and second-magnitude classifications, GPS coordinates, and flow data.
- Many Florida springs are gauged by USGS NWIS — you can pull historical flow data going back decades. This is a unique advantage of the region.

## Watch-outs specific to this region

- **Conservation easements** — Florida Forever, water-management-district programs, and private land trusts have placed easements on many spring-bearing tracts. These typically permanently bar development and may bar agriculture too. Easement = G4 fail.
- **Hurricane exposure** — coastal panhandle parcels can be devastated by Gulf hurricanes. Operational risk, not a gate.
- **Water-management-district authority** — even unzoned parcels can be subject to WMD rules on water use and wetland disturbance. Get a WMD compliance letter for any spring-bearing parcel.
- **Karst hazards** — sinkholes can open suddenly in heavily-pumped areas. Building siting requires geotechnical work.
- **Florida sunshine laws** — anything you record (an LLC, an offer) becomes public quickly. Plan accordingly if anonymity matters.

## When to actually pursue this region

Only after the Ozarks and Cumberland Plateau searches have been exhausted, **or** if the principal decides springs are the only thing that matters and budget can flex above $4M, **or** if a smaller-than-1,000-acre tract becomes acceptable in exchange for first-magnitude spring quality.
