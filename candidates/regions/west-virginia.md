# West Virginia / Southwest Virginia Appalachians

**Priority: 4**

## Why this region

Cheapest large land per acre in the eastern U.S., with widespread springs from fractured-rock aquifers. The catch is the region's history of coal, oil, and gas extraction — severed mineral estates are the rule rather than the exception, which makes G5 the binding constraint.

## Geology

Folded and faulted Paleozoic sedimentary rock — sandstone, shale, limestone, and coal. Springs emerge from fractured sandstone and limestone units along valley walls. The Allegheny Plateau (most of WV) and Ridge and Valley (eastern WV, western VA) both produce reliable springs in their respective bedrock conditions.

Annual rainfall: 40–50 inches.

## Zoning posture

West Virginia counties are mostly unzoned at the county level. SW Virginia counties are more variable; verify per parcel.

## Mineral rights climate — the binding constraint

**This is the hardest region in scope to satisfy G5.** Severed mineral estates have been common since the 1880s. Coal, oil, and gas rights are routinely held separately from the surface, sometimes by parties whose identity is itself unclear (heirs of original 19th-century lessees).

The dominant-mineral-estate doctrine in WV historically allowed mineral owners to disturb the surface to extract minerals. Reforms have narrowed this somewhat, but it has not been eliminated. **Buying a surface estate without minerals here means accepting that someone else can drill or mine.**

For any candidate in this region:
1. Mineral title opinion from a WV (or VA) attorney who specializes in mineral title is mandatory. Expect to pay $2,000–$5,000 — more than in other regions because the title chains are longer and more tangled.
2. If minerals are severed, **walk away** unless the seller or a separate party can convey them along with the surface.
3. Check for active drilling permits and pipeline corridors on and adjacent to the parcel.

The Marcellus and Utica shale plays make active drilling a real and growing risk in WV — particularly in northern and western WV and the Ohio River corridor.

## Spring inventory

- WV Geological and Economic Survey publishes geologic and water-resources reports by county.
- USGS NHD covers springs.

Springs in WV are smaller than karst springs but plentiful. Most farms historically had at least one. A 1,000-acre tract in the right sub-region likely has multiple springs; the question is always reliability and mineral title.

## Watch-outs specific to this region

- **Severed minerals** — see above; this is the region's defining issue.
- **Old mining sites** — strip mines, longwall mines, abandoned underground mines. Subsidence, acid mine drainage, and water-table disruption can extend across property lines.
- **Pipelines** — Mountain Valley Pipeline and other gas pipelines have crossed many parcels under eminent domain. Check easement records.
- **Steepness** — much of WV is too steep for crops. G8 (organic crop production) requires identifying the specific arable acreage; many "1,000-acre" WV tracts have only 50–100 tillable acres.
- **Hunt clubs** — many WV tracts have leased hunt-club access. These leases sometimes survive sale; check carefully.
