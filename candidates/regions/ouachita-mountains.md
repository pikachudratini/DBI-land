# Ouachita Mountains (Western Arkansas, Southeastern Oklahoma)

**Priority: 3**

## Why this region

A separate uplift south of the Ozarks, with similar low land prices and weak regulation but different geology. Springs are smaller and somewhat less reliable than Ozark karst springs, but year-round springs do exist. The region is sparsely populated and tracts of 1,000+ acres come up regularly.

## Geology

Folded Paleozoic sandstone, shale, and chert. Unlike the karst Ozarks, the Ouachitas are **fractured-rock** spring country — water moves through fractures and bedding planes rather than dissolved cavities. Springs are smaller (gallons per minute, not millions per day) but well-fractured sandstone units (Jackfork, Stanley) can sustain year-round flow.

Annual rainfall: 50–60 inches (higher than the Ozarks; helps spring reliability).

Budget of $4M can buy 1,500–3,500 acres in this region.

## Zoning posture

Most counties are unzoned. Verify with planning department for any specific parcel.

## Mineral rights climate

Mixed. The Arkoma Basin (along the southern flank of the Ouachitas, especially in Le Flore and Latimer counties OK and parts of Sebastian and Logan AR) has active natural-gas production from the Fayetteville and Woodford shales. Severed minerals are common in those counties.

The core Ouachita uplift counties (Polk, Montgomery, Scott, Yell in AR; Pushmataha, McCurtain in OK) have less hydrocarbon production and cleaner mineral title generally — but always order a mineral title opinion.

## Spring inventory

Less well-catalogued than the Ozarks. The USGS NHD layer will show springs; the AR Geological Survey *Springs of Arkansas* covers the Ouachita counties as well. Hot Springs (city) is known for thermal springs but they are protected federal property — not a candidate area.

Smaller springs in this region are often unnamed and not on official inventories. A good site visit (and ideally a hydrogeologist) matters more here than in the Ozarks because the public data is thinner.

## Watch-outs specific to this region

- **Pine plantations** — much of the region is industrial timberland. Soil compaction from heavy harvest equipment, herbicide application history, and monoculture root systems can disqualify parcels for organic certification (G8). Look for natural hardwood or mixed timber.
- **Natural-gas pipelines** — Arkoma Basin pipelines cross many parcels; both the easement (G4) and the line itself (G7 in some cases) can disqualify.
- **Black bears, drug enforcement aerial surveillance** — practical operational notes, not gates, but worth knowing about for site visits.
- **Spring reliability** — verify dry-season flow more carefully here than in karst country. Some Ouachita springs are seasonal.
